## 1. Kate M. Bourne — University of Calgary, Cumming School of Medicine

- **Why picked:** Bourne is the lead investigator (with Dr. Satish Raj) behind the two compression-garment/POTS trials cited on the site's POTS page — a 2021 JACC tilt-table RCT (n=30) and a newer 2025/2026 abdominal-only compression study (n=25). Her research is the most specific, data-heavy citation on that page, and the page quotes her trial's actual numbers.
- **Contact found:** autonomic.research@ucalgary.ca (listed as the contact for her POTS compression-garment clinical trial, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04881318; the PI is Dr. Satish Raj, Cumming School of Medicine).
- **Page referenced:** [POTS, Dizziness, and Orthostatic Intolerance in Hypermobility](https://hypermobilitysupport.com/pots-adjacent-dizziness/)

**Subject: Your compression garment trial data, on a patient-facing hEDS/POTS page**

Hi Kate,

I write a small, citation-heavy reference site for people with hypermobile EDS and HSD (hypermobilitysupport.com — started after a Reddit post, now at 6,000+ views). On the POTS/dizziness page, I pulled the actual numbers from your 2021 JACC trial — heart rate during tilt averaging 109±19 bpm with no compression versus 92±14 bpm with full abdominal-and-leg compression — because it's one of the only pieces of hard data I could find that tells someone *how much* compression actually blunts the tachycardia, rather than just "compression garments may help."

I also referenced the newer abdominal-only compression study, since a lot of readers ask whether they really need full-leg compression or whether waist-high is enough.

No ask here beyond: if I've represented either study inaccurately, I'd genuinely like to know and will correct it immediately. And if your clinic or the Libin Cardiovascular Institute ever maintains a patient-facing resource list, this page might be a useful specific-enough plain-language companion to point people to before they read the primary literature. Either way, thank you for running trials that actually report the numbers — it makes pages like mine possible to write honestly.

Best,
Maren Voss

## 2. Dr. Jessica Eccles — Brighton and Sussex Medical School

- **Why picked:** Eccles is the corresponding author on Csecs et al. 2022 (Frontiers in Psychiatry, n=109), which is the central study on the site's ADHD/autism/hypermobility page — the 51%-vs-20% generalized joint hypermobility prevalence figure comes directly from that paper. She's also a leading public voice connecting hypermobility to neurodivergence (Royal College of Psychiatrists Neurodevelopmental Psychiatry SIG chair), so this page sits squarely in her own outreach area.
- **Contact found:** J.Eccles@bsms.ac.uk (BSMS staff profile, Reader/Associate Professor in Brain-Body Medicine, Trafford Centre for Medical Research).
- **Page referenced:** [The Link Between ADHD, Autism, and Hypermobility](https://hypermobilitysupport.com/adhd-autism-hypermobility-link/)

**Subject: Citing your 2022 Frontiers in Psychiatry paper on a hypermobility/neurodivergence page**

Hi Dr. Eccles,

I run a small evidence-based reference site on hEDS/HSD (hypermobilitysupport.com), and the page on ADHD, autism, and hypermobility leans heavily on your 2022 Frontiers in Psychiatry paper with Csecs and colleagues — specifically the 51% generalized joint hypermobility rate in the neurodivergent group versus 20% in the general population and 17.5% in the age-matched comparison group, and the finding that joint count itself tracked with dysautonomia severity. That last point is the one I most wanted readers to see, since most popular coverage of the hypermobility-ADHD link stops at "they're correlated" without mentioning the dose-response piece.

The page is aimed at people who suspect the overlap applies to them or their kid and want something more specific than a Reddit thread to bring to a GP. Given that it's really your paper's finding doing the work, I wanted you to see how it's being used, and to flag it in case there's a nuance I've flattened — happy to correct anything.

If it's ever useful, the site also has pages on the dysautonomia and anxiety side of hEDS/HSD that touch adjacent territory to your brain-body work, in case any of them are worth pointing patients or trainees to.

Best,
Maren Voss
hypermobilitysupport.com

## 4. Hypermobility Syndromes Association (HMSA) — UK charity

- **Why picked:** HMSA is a long-running, UK-registered hypermobility-specific charity (not EDS-general) with its own education-resources page, a helpline, and professional-education outreach — a natural fit for a plain-language self-assessment page rather than a research citation. This is the patient-organization pick for this cycle; the Ehlers-Danlos Society itself was considered but is large enough that an unsolicited note is less likely to be useful than to a smaller, UK-focused charity actively building out its resources page.
- **Contact found:** info@hypermobility.org (general charity contact, published on the Charity Commission register and HMSA's own contact page).
- **Page referenced:** [The Beighton Score: Hypermobility Self-Assessment Test](https://hypermobilitysupport.com/beighton-score-self-assessment/)

**Subject: A self-assessment page that might suit your education resources**

Hi HMSA team,

I run a small, citation-based reference site on hEDS/HSD called Hypermobility Support (hypermobilitysupport.com) — it started from a single Reddit post and has had 6,000+ views since, mostly from people trying to figure out whether what they're feeling has a name before they get anywhere near a diagnosis.

One page walks through the Beighton score itself — the five maneuvers, the age-adjusted thresholds (5/9 in adults, 6/9 in prepubescent children, 4/9 over 50), and the "historical hypermobility" question that a lot of people don't know is part of the 2017 diagnostic criteria. Every claim on it is sourced back to the original papers and the Society's own Beighton Score reference PDF, which I also cite directly.

I noticed your education-resources page already links out to a few external tools and toolkits, and this felt like it might be a reasonable plain-language companion for someone who wants to do the self-test before they even know to search for "Beighton score" by name. No pressure either way — happy to send more detail or make any correction you flag, and thank you for the helpline and support-group work you do regardless.

Best,
Maren Voss
hypermobilitysupport.com
